There is no recording of a Roman. Everything below is reconstruction — good
reconstruction, from converging evidence, but reconstruction, and this page says
so at the top rather than in a footnote. A learner is entitled to know that the
sound of the language they are being taught is an inference.

## 1. Two traditions, and why you must choose

Two pronunciations are in respectable use, and they are not two accents of one
system; they are the sound of the language at two different points in its life.

**Restored classical** is the reconstruction of educated Roman speech around the
first century BC. It is what the evidence in §2 supports, it is what makes
Latin verse scan, and it is the pronunciation this course teaches by default.

**Ecclesiastical**, sometimes called Italianate or Church Latin, is the
pronunciation of the Roman liturgy, essentially Latin read with the phonology of
Italian. It is not a mistake. It is the living pronunciation of a continuous
tradition, it is what a singer needs for a Mass setting, and it is what most
Latin actually said aloud in the last fifteen hundred years has sounded like.

Choose one and be consistent. Reading *Caesar* as `KAI-sar` in one line and
`CHAY-sar` in the next teaches nothing.

## 2. What the reconstruction rests on

- **Roman grammarians describing their own language.** Quintilian, Varro,
  Terentianus Maurus and the later *grammatici* discuss letters, quantities and
  sounds directly, sometimes to complain about people getting them wrong.
- **Verse.** Latin metre is quantitative. A hexameter only scans if the vowel
  quantities are what the reconstruction says they are; the corpus of surviving
  verse is therefore an enormous, self-checking dataset of vowel length.
- **Misspellings.** Graffiti, curse tablets and unlettered inscriptions record
  what writers heard rather than what they were taught, and the mistakes are
  systematic.
- **Transcription into and out of Greek.** Greek had a different alphabet and its
  own well-attested phonology; how Romans wrote Greek names, and how Greeks wrote
  Roman ones, constrains the possibilities sharply.
- **The Romance languages.** What Latin became in Spain, Gaul, Italy and Dacia
  limits what it can have been, and dates the changes.

## 3. Vowels

Five vowel qualities, each long or short. Length is phonemic — see
[orthography and macrons](/appendices/orthography-and-macrons/).

| Letter | Restored classical | Ecclesiastical | English approximation (classical) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `a` | short open front-central | as classical | *cup*, but fronter |
| `ā` | the same quality, held longer | as classical | *father* |
| `e` | short mid front | as classical | *pet* |
| `ē` | long close-mid front | as classical | *they*, without the glide |
| `i` | short near-close front | as classical | *pit* |
| `ī` | long close front | as classical | *machine* |
| `o` | short mid back rounded | as classical | *pot* |
| `ō` | long close-mid back | as classical | *note*, without the glide |
| `u` | short near-close back rounded | as classical | *put* |
| `ū` | long close back | as classical | *rude* |
| `y`, `ȳ` | front rounded, as Greek *upsilon* | as `i` | French *tu* |

The essential point, and the one worth more than the whole table: **long vowels
differ from short vowels in duration, and a long vowel is not a different
letter.** English speakers substitute quality for quantity by reflex and must be
told not to.

## 4. Diphthongs

| Spelling | Restored classical | Ecclesiastical |
|---|---|---|
| `ae` | as English *eye* | as `e` in *pet* |
| `oe` | as English *boy* | as `e` in *pet* |
| `au` | as English *how* | as classical |
| `eu` | *e* + *u* glided in one syllable | as classical |
| `ei`, `ui` | as written, in one syllable | as classical |

The `ae` and `oe` rows are the loudest single difference between the two
traditions. *Caesar* is `KAI-sar` in restored classical and `CHE-sar` in
ecclesiastical; *poena* is `POI-na` against `PE-na`.

## 5. Consonants

Only the consonants that differ from English expectation are listed.

| Letter | Restored classical | Ecclesiastical |
|---|---|---|
| `c` | always hard, as *k*, before every vowel | *ch* as in *church* before `e`, `i`, `ae`, `oe`; otherwise hard |
| `g` | always hard, as in *get* | *j* as in *gem* before `e`, `i`, `ae`, `oe` |
| `v` | as English *w* | as English *v* |
| `i` (consonantal) | as English *y* | as English *y* |
| `s` | always voiceless, as in *sit* | voiced between vowels |
| `r` | trilled or tapped | trilled or tapped |
| `t` | always as in *stop*, never aspirated | *ts* in the sequence `ti` + vowel |
| `gn` | `ŋn`, as in *hangnail* | as Italian *gn* in *signore* |
| `h` | breathed, weakly | silent |
| `ch`, `ph`, `th` | aspirated stops, as Greek *chi*, *phi*, *theta* | `ch` as *k*, `ph` as *f*, `th` as *t* |
| `x` | `ks` | `ks` |
| doubled consonants | genuinely held longer, as in Italian | as classical |

*Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* is `WAY-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee` restored, and
`VAY-nee VEE-dee VEE-chee` ecclesiastical. Nothing else makes the divergence so
audible in so few words.

## 6. Syllables

Divide the word into syllables before applying the stress rule; almost every
misplaced Latin accent is a syllable-division error rather than a rule error.

1. A single consonant between vowels goes with the following vowel:
   *a-mī-cus*, *Rō-ma*.
2. Two consonants divide between them: *ma-gis-ter*, *puel-la*.
3. A stop followed by `l` or `r` normally stays together: *pa-tris*, *a-grī* —
   though in verse the poet may divide it, which is a licence, not an error.
4. `ch`, `ph`, `th`, `qu` and `gu` count as one consonant and are never split:
   *a-qua*, *phi-lo-so-phus*.
5. Compounds divide at the seam: *ab-est*, *trans-eō*.

**A long syllable is not the same as a long vowel.** A syllable is long if it
contains a long vowel or a diphthong (long *by nature*), or if its vowel is
followed by two or more consonants (long *by position*). The first `a` of
*magister* is short, but the syllable *ma-gis-* is heavy at *gis*. This
distinction does no work until the stress rule below, and then it does all of it.

## 7. Stress

Latin stress is fully predictable and never marked in writing.

- **Two syllables:** stress the first. *Rō-ma*, *ter-ra*.
- **Three or more:** look at the **penultimate** syllable — the second from the
  end. If it is long, stress it. If it is short, stress the one before it.

So *a-**mī**-cus* (penult long by nature) but ***fē**-mi-na* (penult short, so
the stress retreats). *Ma-**gis**-ter* is stressed on the penult because it is
long by position, even though its vowel is short — the case in which the
distinction of §6 earns its keep.

The rule is identical in both pronunciation traditions. It is the single most
useful thing on this page: it is exceptionless in classical Latin, and it lets a
learner read any unfamiliar word aloud correctly on sight.

## 8. Audio

This course ships **no synthetic Latin audio**.

The open text-to-speech voices used by the sister courses in this family are
trained on recordings of native speakers. Latin has none, and the collection
contains no Latin voice. An Italian or Spanish voice can be made to produce
something ecclesiastical-adjacent, but it would be an approximation of a
tradition presented as a model, which is exactly the confusion this page exists
to prevent.

Where audio is added later it will be **curated, openly-licensed human
readings** in the restored classical pronunciation, for a small set of showcase
passages, labelled as reconstructed and attributed to their readers. Nothing on
this page depends on it: the tables and the stress rule are written so that a
learner working in silence can still read aloud correctly and consistently.
