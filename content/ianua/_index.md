*Ianua* is the doorway: the short onboarding stage a Latin course needs in place
of the script-literacy stage its sister courses need for Arabic, Japanese or
Russian. The alphabet is already yours. What is not yours yet is vowel length,
the syllable, the stress rule, and the knowledge that there are two defensible
ways to pronounce this language and that you must choose one knowingly.

## Why a Level 0 at all

Latin costs a learner of a European language almost nothing at the level of
letters, and a great deal at the level of quantity. Vowel length is phonemic:
*liber* is a book, *līber* is free; *populus* is a people, *pōpulus* is a poplar.
It is also grammatical, and this is the part that catches learners out for
years: in the first declension, *puella* (nominative) and *puellā* (ablative) are
the same six letters and different cases, distinguished in speech by the length
of the final vowel alone. A learner who never hears or marks that distinction is
obliged to recover the case from context on every single line, forever.

So this stage teaches the mark before it teaches the grammar that depends on it.
Throughout the course, teaching text and vocabulary carry macrons — `ā ē ī ō ū ȳ`
— and quoted inscriptions and manuscript readings do not, because there fidelity
matters more. The full policy, including the treatment of consonantal *u/v*, of
*i*, and of the *ae/oe* digraphs, is set out in the appendix on
[orthography and macrons](/appendices/orthography-and-macrons/).

## What you should be able to do at the end

- Read any Latin word aloud from its written form, consistently, in the
  tradition you have chosen.
- Place the accent of a word of three or more syllables without being told.
- Say what a macron marks, and what it does not mark.
- Recognise that *puella* and *puellā* are two forms, and hear the difference.

{{< callout type="note" title="Pronunciation and audio" >}}
No native speaker survives to be recorded, and no Latin voice exists in the
open text-to-speech collections this project uses for its other languages. The
course therefore ships **no synthetic Latin audio**. Where audio appears later it
will be curated openly-licensed human readings in the restored classical
pronunciation, labelled as reconstructed. The reference tables in the appendix on
[pronunciation](/appendices/pronunciation/) are written to be usable without any
audio at all.
