## Objectives

- *I can name the letters of the Latin alphabet and say which letters of the
  English alphabet are not among them.*
- *I can say what a macron marks, and give a pair of words that differ only in
  vowel length.*
- *I can read the six diphthongs aloud in restored classical pronunciation.*
- *I can name the five consonants whose Latin value differs from my
  expectation, and read a short word list aloud consistently.*

## 1. Activate

**Two words, one difference.** Write these two words on the board and say
nothing else:

> **liber**  ·  **līber**

Ask what the bar might be for. Collect answers before giving one. Most classes
propose stress, or a long-ago abbreviation, or decoration. The answer — that the
bar means *hold this vowel longer*, and that holding it longer changes *book*
into *free* — is worth more when it has been guessed at first.

Then the harder pair, which is the real reason for this whole stage:

> **puella** *a girl*, as the subject  ·  **puellā** *by a girl*, *with a girl*

Same six letters. Different case. In speech the Romans heard the difference;
in unmarked print you cannot see it. This course marks it.

## 2. Input

### 2.1 The alphabet

Classical Latin uses **twenty-three letters**:

> A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X Y Z

Three observations, and they matter more than the list:

- **There is no `J` and no `W`.** The letter `I` does double duty: a vowel in
  *ītur*, a consonant with the value of English *y* in *iam*, *Iūlius*,
  *iaciō*. This course never writes `j`; see the
  [orthography appendix](/appendices/orthography-and-macrons/).
- **`U` and `V` were one letter.** The Romans wrote V for both the vowel and the
  consonant. Modern teaching editions, including this one, split them: `u` for
  the vowel, `v` for the consonant. *SERVVS* on a stone is *servus* on this
  page.
- **`K`, `Y` and `Z` are marginal.** `K` survives in a handful of words
  (*Kalendae*, *Karthāgō*); `Y` and `Z` were imported to spell Greek loanwords
  (*lȳra*, *zōna*) and are pronounced as Greek.

### 2.2 The five vowels, each long and short

Each vowel has one quality and two lengths. The macron marks the long one.

| Short | Long | Long is held | Contrast pair |
|---|---|---|---|
| `a` | `ā` | roughly twice as long | *malum* evil · *mālum* apple |
| `e` | `ē` | " | *levis* light · *lēvis* smooth |
| `i` | `ī` | " | *vir* man · *vīr-* in *vīrēs* strength |
| `o` | `ō` | " | *populus* a people · *pōpulus* a poplar |
| `u` | `ū` | " | *fugit* he flees · *fūgit* he fled |
| `y` | `ȳ` | Greek only | *tyrannus* · *lȳra* |

{{< callout type="warning" title="The English reflex to unlearn" >}}
English distinguishes vowels by **quality** — *bit* and *beat* are different
sounds, not the same sound held for different times. Latin distinguishes them by
**quantity**: the same sound, longer. An English speaker will substitute quality
for quantity by reflex, and must be told, repeatedly, in the first three units,
that *ā* is *a* held, not *a* changed.
{{< /callout >}}

### 2.3 The diphthongs

A diphthong is two vowel letters read as one syllable. There are six; the first
two carry almost all the traffic.

| Written | Restored classical value | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `ae` | as English *eye* | *Caesar*, *puellae* |
| `oe` | as English *boy* | *poena*, *proelium* |
| `au` | as English *how* | *aurum*, *causa* |
| `eu` | *e* and *u* glided together | *Eurōpa*, *heu* |
| `ei` | as English *day* | *deinde* |
| `ui` | *u* and *i* glided together | *huic*, *cui* |

Where two vowels are *not* a diphthong, the second is written with its own
macron or a diaeresis, so that *po-ē-ta* is three syllables and never rhymes with
*Caesar*.

### 2.4 The consonants that are not what you expect

Everything not listed here has its English value.

| Letter | Value | Say it |
|---|---|---|
| `c` | **always** hard, as *k*, before every vowel | *Cicerō* is `KI-ke-ro` |
| `g` | **always** hard, as in *get* | *gēns*, *regō* |
| `v` | as English *w* | *vīvit* is `WEE-wit` |
| `i` before a vowel | as English *y* | *iam* is `yam` |
| `s` | **always** voiceless, as in *sit*, never as in *rose* | *rosa* is `RO-sa` |
| `r` | tapped or trilled, never English | *Rōma* |
| `ch ph th` | aspirated *k*, *p*, *t* — Greek letters, not English digraphs | *pulcher*, *philosophus* |
| `gn` | `ŋn`, as in *hangnail* | *magnus* |
| doubled | genuinely held longer, as in Italian | *puella*, *terra* |

*Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* — `WAY-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee`. If a class remembers one thing
from this unit, this is the one that will make them remember the rest.

The ecclesiastical values, which differ in most of these rows, are set out
column by column in the [pronunciation appendix](/appendices/pronunciation/).
This unit teaches restored classical; Unit 3 puts the two traditions side by
side so that the choice is made in the open.

## 3. Practise

### Support

1. Which four letters of the English alphabet are missing from classical Latin?
2. Read aloud: *Rōma · rosa · casa · silva · aqua.*
3. Mark the diphthong in each: *aurum · poena · Caesar · Eurōpa.*

### Core

4. Read each pair aloud so that a listener could tell them apart:
   *malum / mālum · liber / līber · populus / pōpulus · fugit / fūgit.*
5. Give the value of `c` in *Cicerō*, *causa*, *cēna*, *cīvis*. What is the rule?
6. Transcribe into the convention of this course, splitting `u` from `v`:
   *SERVVS · IVLIVS · VIVIT · AVXILIVM.*

### Extension

7. *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* has been read aloud in the ecclesiastical tradition for
   most of its life, and in the restored classical tradition for about a
   century. Write both readings in your own respelling, then say in two
   sentences which you will use in this course and why. There is no wrong
   answer; there is an inconsistent one.

{{< details title="Answer key" type="tip" >}}
**1.** `J`, `U` as a separate letter from `V`, `W`, and — if the class counts it
— nothing else; `K`, `Y`, `Z` are present but rare. Accept *J* and *W* as the
core answer.

**2.** `RO-ma` (single tapped *r*, voiceless *s* not applicable), `RO-sa` with
*s* as in *sit*, `KA-sa`, `SIL-wa` (`v` = *w*), `A-kwa`.

**3.** *au*rum · p*oe*na · C*ae*sar · *Eu*rōpa.

**4.** Listen for duration, not for a change of vowel colour. If a pupil says
*mah-lum* against *may-lum*, they have substituted quality for quantity — correct
it now, not later.

**5.** Hard *k* in all four. The rule: `c` in classical Latin is invariably
hard, whatever follows. The soft *ch* of *Cicero* belongs to the ecclesiastical
tradition.

**6.** *servus · Iūlius · vīvit · auxilium.* Note that `I` in *Iūlius* stays `I`:
this course does not write `J`. Macrons here are editorial; a stone carries
none.

**7.** Restored: `WAY-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee`. Ecclesiastical:
`VAY-nee VEE-dee VEE-chee`. Accept any defended choice; mark the consistency,
not the preference.
{{< /details >}}

## 4. Apply

**Reading a stone.** Below is the opening of a funerary inscription in the form
it is actually cut — capitals, no word spacing in the original, no macrons, `V`
for both *u* and *v*. It is reproduced without macrons deliberately: this is the
one context in which this course does not mark vowel length.

> **D · M · IVLIAE · FELICVLAE · CONIVGI · CARISSIMAE**

1. Rewrite it in the convention of this course: lower case, `u` split from `v`,
   word spacing kept, macrons **not** added (you do not yet know the
   quantities).
2. `D · M` is the standard opening abbreviation *Dīs Mānibus*, "to the spirits
   of the dead". Every one of the remaining four words ends in the same two
   letters. What might that ending be doing?
3. You are not expected to translate this. You are expected to notice that the
   endings are doing grammatical work — which is the whole subject of Stage I.

{{< details title="Answer key" type="tip" >}}
**1.** *D · M · Iuliae Feliculae coniugi carissimae.* The `V` of *IVLIAE* and
*FELICVLAE* is a vowel and becomes `u`; there is no consonantal V in this text.

**2.** All four are dative singular — the inscription is dedicated *to* Iulia
Felicula, *to* a most beloved wife. Accept any answer that recognises the
endings as marking a shared function rather than as a coincidence of spelling.

**3.** No translation expected. The point is the observation.
{{< /details >}}

## 5. Reflect

- [ ] I can list the letters classical Latin does not have.
- [ ] I can explain what a macron marks — and that it is not stress.
- [ ] I can read *malum* and *mālum* so that a listener can tell them apart.
- [ ] I can read the six diphthongs aloud.
- [ ] I can give the classical value of `c`, `g`, `v`, `s` and consonantal `i`.

**One line in your notebook.** Write the pair *puella / puellā*, and beside it,
in your own words, why this course prints the bar.

<div class="notes">
**Timing (90 minutes).** Activate 8 min · Input 2.1–2.2 20 min · Input 2.3–2.4
20 min · Practise 20 min · Apply 15 min · Reflect 7 min. For two single lessons,
break after Input 2.2 and re-open with the *malum/mālum* pair.

**Differentiation — support.** Give the practise items 1–3 only, and supply the
respelling column of the consonant table already filled in. The measurable
outcome is reading a five-word list aloud consistently, not describing the
system.

**Extension.** Item 7 above. A stronger pupil can be given the *Cicerō /
Ciceró* problem directly: ask them to find, in the pronunciation appendix, the
piece of evidence that decides it — Greek transcription of Roman names with
kappa — and to explain in three sentences why that evidence is decisive.

**Common pitfalls.**

- Macron read as stress. It is not; stress is predictable and unmarked, and is
  the subject of Unit 2.
- `v` read as English *v*. Persistent; correct every time in the first three
  units or it sets.
- `ae` read as `ay` on the model of Latin taught in English schools before the
  restoration. Anchor it to *eye* and to *Caesar*.
- Doubled consonants read as single. *puella* has a genuinely long `l`; this
  becomes audible and metrically necessary at Stage IV.
